Project capacity & scope analysis

Diagnosing why a client feels “no progress” while the team logs plenty of hours.

A case exercise built on a realistic project scenario, not a real client engagement. Team members are referred to by their roles.

The short version

My hypothesis: it's not an underperforming team. The “no progress” feeling comes from a mid-sprint scope change hitting reduced capacity and probable spillover, while one-sided communication hid the trade-offs from the client. What I'd do tomorrow: lightweight client checkpoints, with each sprint plan shared up front, goals set together two sprints ahead and the client invited to Sprint Reviews.

01 The situation

Plenty of hours, yet the client feels stalled.

A four-person team is one month into a six-month build, run as two sprints of two weeks each. The client flags missed deadlines. The team says it is “working as planned”. In the second sprint, the client changed the scope. Around 524 hours were logged that month.

The dip isn't the story

A drop in week two is just a public holiday. The real issue is hidden in the totals: 64 of the mid developer's 152 logged hours were sick leave and planned vacation, not project work, and nobody on the team absorbed the gap.

02 What worried me

Three red flags.

  1. An unplanned absence nobody backfilled

    The mid developer's sick leave in sprint one wasn't absorbed by the team: nobody else's hours went up to cover it. That most likely pushed work into sprint two.

  2. A scope change in the middle of a sprint

    New scope landed inside sprint two, which was already planned, with tasks assigned in advance and capacity reduced by a planned vacation. There was no room to deliver it that cycle.

  3. A one-sided communication channel

    Every client meeting runs through a single developer. The designer, more than 100 hours in, has none. The client sees the project through one narrow, technical lens.

03 My hypothesis

It's not an underperforming team.

The “no progress” feeling comes from a mid-sprint scope change hitting reduced capacity and probable spillover, while one-sided communication hid the trade-offs from the client.

What supports it

  • The sick leave wasn't covered: nobody else's hours went up, so work likely spilled over.
  • Sprint two had reduced capacity because of a planned vacation.
  • The scope change came mid-sprint, with no room to absorb it.
  • All client communication flows through one person.

What challenges it

  • There is no data on how the change was negotiated, so other explanations are possible.
  • Raising the alarm after one month of six may mean high expectations.
  • Hours are logged in broad categories, so real output is hard to verify.

04 To confirm or disprove

Questions I'd ask.

  1. What has been delivered so far, and how do the sprint-two change requests relate to it?

    Team

  2. Was there spillover from sprint one into sprint two?

    Team

  3. How was the scope change agreed, and was its impact on the schedule established?

    Team + client

  4. Which deadlines were missed, and how do they map to the original six-month plan?

    Client

05 What I'd do tomorrow

Lightweight client checkpoints.

  • Share each sprint's plan with the client up front.
  • Set goals together, two sprints ahead.
  • Invite the client to Sprint Reviews to see progress first-hand.

Problem it solves

  • Both sides stay aligned on status and timelines.
  • Fewer mid-sprint scope changes, because the client has a proper channel for them.
  • The client feels progress because they see it.

New risk it adds

  • A client who co-plans ahead may get over-involved or start micromanaging.
  • If they lack time, the fallback is an email with the plan and the outcome, plus one joint goal-setting session every two sprints.

06 How I'd tell the client

The message.

Hi [name],

Thanks for sharing your feedback on the pace of delivery. I'd like to walk you through what happened this past month. During sprint two the team was working with reduced availability, which coincided with the scope change you raised in that same sprint, so we couldn't fully fold the new requirements into that cycle. Based on our current assessment, the six-month timeline remains achievable.

Starting next sprint, we're introducing regular checkpoints: we'll share the sprint plan in advance, set goals together two sprints ahead, and invite you to our Sprint Reviews, so you'll see progress first-hand and have room to raise changes. Could we start this week?

Klaudia

→ Takeaway

Turning complexity into clarity.

Progress isn't only about hours logged. It's about making that progress visible, and giving the client a seat at the table before problems surface.
